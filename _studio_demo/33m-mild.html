A FRAGMENT, RESET IN THE STUDIO'S TYPE

Where I come from,
we have thirty-three million
gods.

The book shouted in chunky sans and arterial red. The studio answers in italic serif and a single red word, and asks you to lean closer rather than stand back. Held at specimen scale by editorial choice.

You needed rain, so you built a god. You needed courage, so you built another. You needed a god to tell you that you were right and the neighbour was wrong, and the neighbour built one too, and the gods argued in the temple while the people, exhausted, slept. None of this was about truth. It was a tooling problem. Thirty-three million instruments for the work of being human.

GODS WEREN'T TRUTH. GODS WERE TOOLS.

The Doomscroller arrives at the end of that long line. He has the shape of a deity and the posture of someone who has been awake too long. His body is patterned the way a god's body is patterned — a consecrated surface, a system of marks meant to be read at a distance — except the marks now record not myth but feed. He does not bless. He scrolls.

I did not set out to build a deity for this century. I set out to notice what the older deities had been doing all along, which was absorbing the burden of a feeling so we could keep moving. Fear of rain, fear of the neighbour, fear of being unwitnessed. Each time, the same operation: name the fear, give it a face, hand the face a weapon, set it down somewhere visible, and walk away lighter.

YOU NEEDED SOMETHING. SO YOU BUILT ONE.

The Doomscroller is the same operation, performed badly. The face is too distracted to bless. The weapon is a thumb. The visible place is the palm. The walk-away-lighter never happens, because the ritual never ends. You feed him because he asks. He asks because you feed him. Devotion has been refactored into engagement, and the temple has very good metrics.

I make him in ceramic because ceramic is what the older gods were made of. I dress him in the patterning of an heirloom because that is the patterning we have inherited. I keep him small because the scale of a thing is part of its argument, and the argument here is that the deity of our attention is, in fact, a small object that fits in the hand.

NO ONE IS WATCHING. NO ONE ELSE RESPONSIBLE.

That is the studio's mild version of the book's loud line. The book could shout it because the book is a paper artefact and you can put the page down. A website cannot shout, because a website is a room you are already standing in. So the studio sets the same sentence in mono caps, indents it, and trusts you to hear the emphasis without the volume.

· · ·

The book ends with a question and the studio inherits it. If the old gods were tools for fear, and the new god is a tool for the absence of fear — for the dull, continuous low-grade ache of always being slightly informed and never quite present — then what do we build next, and who builds it, and on whose authority. I do not have the answer. I have the figurine.

CLOSING LINE

I make gods,
and the systems they live in.
