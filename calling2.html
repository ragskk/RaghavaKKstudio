Calling All Gods.

Two paintings, each twelve and a half feet high and twenty-five feet wide, stand side by side as one unbroken wall. From across the room they look like devotion: vast, intricate, more than the eye can hold. Walk closer and the gods start to misbehave: cartoons, memes, historical figures, villains, the faces nobody admits to praying to.

§ 7Artist Statement

I went looking for God and found a list instead. It was not a dignified list.

When my marriage ended and I was flat on my back in a desert, unable to stand, I did not call on the God I believe in. I called on everyone. Krishna. Jesus. Hanuman. Donald Duck. My own rage. Whatever was within reach.

That should embarrass me. It doesn't anymore, and that is the hopeful part.

Because here is what I learned face down in the dust. You are not who you believe you are. You are who you reach for when you are breaking*. And that crowd you reach for is a circus. Gods and cartoons. Saints and villains. The Bhagavad Gita and a video game. The holy ones you would name in public, and the ones you would never admit to, the rage and the revenge and the denial, sitting right there in the same hand.

I painted all of them. With the same love. Because that is honestly how we use them**.

And you do it too. Everyone alive right now is calling on something to get through this century, the whole species reaching at once for the most ridiculous and magnificent collection of gods imaginable. You are not broken for it. You are gloriously, hilariously human, in a time that gives no one solid ground.

So the painting does not ask you to clean up your list. You can't, and you shouldn't. It does not ask you to resolve yourself into one tidy person. There is no such person, and they would be unbearable company. It asks one thing. Hold it. The whole contradictory, sacred, embarrassing pile. The god and the devil and the duck. And keep standing.

That is the woman on the broken ground. She is not free. She is not winning. She is holding everything that will not reconcile, with what I am fairly sure is a small smile, and she has not fallen.

Neither have you. That is great news. Because the alternative to held is not resolution. It is violence or collapse.

Raghava KK · Bangalore · 2026

Two notes, expanded ↓

*You are who you reach for when you are breakingOpen →

Plainly: in normal life you can curate yourself. You choose your words, your values, the image you present. So who you "are" looks like a decision you make. But when something breaks you, a death, a divorce, a war, real fear, you don't have time to curate. You reach. Automatically. For whatever actually steadies you. And that reflex shows what you genuinely run on, not what you claim to.

A few plain examples. Someone says they're rational and secular, then their kid gets sick and they find themselves praying. Someone says they forgive, then they get hurt and reach straight for revenge. Someone preaches calm, then panics and reaches for control. The crisis strips the performance and shows the actual operating system underneath. When you were breaking, which of these did you actually grab? That one is you.

**Because that is honestly how we use themOpen →

It means the love in the painting is not Raghava approving of these gods. It is him being accurate about how we actually treat them.

In real life we don't keep our ugly impulses at arm's length. We don't reach for revenge reluctantly and Krishna warmly. We reach for both the same way: with full intensity, total intimacy, complete dependence. When someone wrongs you, the fantasy of revenge is not cold and clinical. It is comforting. You curl up in it. You feed it. You return to it like a shrine. That is devotion. Same with denial, with rage, with ego. We treat them like gods because that is functionally what they are to us, things we turn to, trust, and let run our lives in the bad moments.

So if Raghava painted the noble gods with love and the dark ones with disgust, he would be lying. He would be showing how we think we relate to them, not how we actually do. The honesty is in the equal treatment. Hitler gets the same brushwork as Hanuman because the man on the floor reaching for certainty, or revenge, or someone to blame, is reaching with the same desperate tenderness he reaches for the holy.

The "same love" is not a moral statement. It is a confession. It admits that we adore our worst gods too. We just don't say it out loud.
