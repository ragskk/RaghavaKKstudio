# About Raghava KK

*The Artist Beyond Boundaries* (studio monograph, 2025)

## The Artist

Don't be deceived by Raghava KK's flamboyant costumes, mellow guruji persona, or boy-next-door demeanor. He is a master thought provocateur, a shape-shifting artist who refuses to be confined to artistic archetypes. His neurodivergence and restless spirit drive him to weave together a fragmented self into a singular, lived narrative, constantly evolving and pushing the limits of what art can be. This relentless pursuit is what makes him one of the most significant and relevant artists of our time. His story is not just of personal triumph, but of a creative force that blurs the edges between life, technology, and artistic expression.

Raghava's art is a world unto itself, an ecosystem where opposing forces dance in chaos and harmony. He believes that an artist should constantly challenge their biases, even if it means reinventing themselves several times.

His ability to break free from rigid definitions has made him a pioneer across multiple disciplines, from painting and performance to AI-driven art and NFT experiments. Through this journey, one theme remains constant: Raghava never creates alone. His work thrives on conversation and collaboration, feeding off the collective energy of scientists, musicians, dancers, technologists, philosophers and even artificial intelligence. His art is not merely something to be viewed; it is something to be lived, questioned, and felt.

## 1996 to 1998: Caricaturing His Way Around the World

Raghava began his career at 16 as a cartoon columnist for The Times of India, The Asian Age, and The Indian Express. His sharp wit and keen social critique quickly gained him recognition. But even in those early years, Raghava was not content with static frames of ink and paper. He wanted to push cartoons beyond newspapers. He convinced companies like Intel, Infosys, and IBM to use cartoons as a tool for communication and founded The Lee 'N' Rags Cartoon Academy, where he taught thousands globally. It was through this initiative that he funded his own travels, sketching his way through the world.

His break into the global stage came in an unexpected way. At just 17, Raghava convinced the Government of India to send him to the World Trade Congress in Strasbourg to caricature international delegates. He then embarked on a shoestring-budget adventure, hitchhiking across Europe, immersing himself in unfamiliar realities. Along the way, he encountered Italian master painter Luigi Ontani, a flamboyant artistic iconoclast who became an early inspiration and mentor. Ontani saw something in Raghava, a raw, untamed creative force, and invited him into his world. Together, they set off to rediscover lost artistic treasures, teaching Raghava the power of narrative in art. Ontani's eccentricity and boundless imagination gave Raghava the permission he needed: to be fully, unapologetically himself.

Through a sequence of serendipitous events, Raghava was invited to exhibit alongside Syed Raza, Shakti Burman, and other Indian master painters at the Indian Embassy in Paris. This led to an extraordinary twist of fate: India's Ambassador to France, Kapil Sibal, offered him a bed in the servant's quarters beneath the embassy. What could have been an indignity became a transformative moment. He realized that art was not merely a craft but an adventure, one that demanded equal parts audacity and humility.

## 1998 to 2001: The Discovery of Painting and Celebrity Fame

Returning to India, Raghava abandoned cartoons and threw himself into painting. His transformation was catalyzed by Akkumal Ramachander, an eccentric art promoter who had recently discovered New York based artist Harold Shapinsky. Akkumal believed that Raghava was his next great find. Under his guidance, Raghava met Milind, whose fearless, instinctive approach to painting revolutionized his perspective [source has a stray period: "instinctive. approach"]. Watching Milind create a masterpiece in mere minutes, Raghava had an epiphany: he had been too careful, too rigid. That night, he painted 15 canvases, unlocking a new, visceral way of working.

This unleashed an unstoppable flood of creativity. Raghava abandoned traditional tools, painting with his hands and feet, treating the canvas as an extension of his own body. His figurative, gestural works pulsed with raw energy, drawing collectors directly to his studio. He followed Flamenco dancers across Spain and southern France, painting them live as master singer Pepe Linares performed on stage, capturing the raw passion of the art form. Within months, he had become an overnight sensation in the Indian art world. MTV and Channel V featured him; society pages chronicled his rise. His work was collected by corporate tycoons, Bollywood celebrities, and industrialists.

Fame came fast, and with it, a warning from a journalist who shaped Bollywood careers: "We make you only to use you, and then we will destroy you. Be smarter about your publicity." Seeking artistic growth beyond India, Raghava saved up enough money to study for a summer at The Parsons School of Design, New York. During a summer program, he was invited by his professor Pauline to join the Robin Hood Foundation's 'Library Initiative', an ambitious project redesigning libraries for underprivileged schools throughout New York City. Working with children from the Bronx, Raghava created murals, his first public installation in the U.S.

Meanwhile, he developed a close creative bond with legendary Indian actor Kamal Haasan, which led to his first performance-art piece, *Anthropomorphism: When Paintings Dance* (2000). The piece, merging movement and painting, was later performed by Velocity Circus in San Francisco, featuring Cirque du Soleil dancers and staged at the high-profile AIF gala hosted by Anne Getty [captions spell the name "Ann Getty"].

## 2001 to 2005: Love, Art and Intellectual Stimulation

In 2001, while living in New York, Raghava met Netra, a fiercely intelligent woman who would become his muse and life partner. Their relationship, filled with intellectual debates and artistic discourse, propelled him forward. Over the next four years, they shuttled between New York and Bangalore, selling paintings along the East Coast of America to fund their adventures. Cambridge, Massachusetts, became a second home, where he found himself in the company of Harvard and MIT scholars. He designed protest posters for liberal student movements and engaged in discussions with figures like Benazir Bhutto, Taslima Nasreen, and Amartya Sen.

By 2005, Netra had completed her studies, and the couple returned to India. But while Raghava's career had flourished internationally, the Indian art world posed a new challenge. His bold, instinctive approach stood apart from the more conceptual, theory-driven trends that dominated the space at the time. Rather than seeing this as a barrier, Raghava embraced the friction, seeing it as an opportunity to expand his own vocabulary. He engaged with the scene on his own terms, blurring the lines between classical influences and contemporary forms, emotion and intellect, chaos and control.

Even as he carved his space in India, he continued to build bridges globally. He collaborated with Moroccan master Farid Belkahia, creating a piece for Princess Lalla Salma of Morocco, and staged performances at Lincoln Center and London Fashion Week.

The Carré d'Art Museum in Nîmes, France, invited him to exhibit a series exploring tradition versus modernity. While in Nîmes, he also gave guest lectures at the École des Beaux-Arts, sharing not just artistic techniques, but the deeply personal philosophies that fueled his constant reinvention.

While critics debated where to place him, collectors had no such hesitation [source has a typographic glitch: "WThile"]. His work, unburdened by categorization, found its way into the collections of the Royal Family of Morocco and Indian icons like Shah Rukh Khan, Amitabh Bachchan, and Sachin Tendulkar. Key collectors of Raghava's work include Paul Simon, Princess Lalla Salma of Morocco, Benazir Bhutto, Asha Jadeja Motwani, Jeff Bezos, and Mike Novogratz.

## 2007 to 2008: The Big Fat Indian Wedding and a Radical New Aesthetic

Raghava and Netra's wedding was an artistic spectacle, attended by 7,000 guests, featuring art installations, and chronicled in a mockumentary and documentary titled *The Big Fat Indian Wedding*, which aired for a decade. The couple arrived at their wedding reception in an auto-rickshaw turned into a chariot (2007).

The extravagance left Raghava drained. Seeking solitude, he retreated to Goa and produced *Not Another Wedding in Goa*, a series where his vibrant aesthetic gave way to graphic, powerful, and darker themes.

His reinvention was solidified when he joined Art Musings, a Mumbai-based gallery, marking his entry into the Mumbai art discourse. Over the next decade, he held over eight solo shows, where his work became whimsical yet laced with darkness, humor intertwined with critique.

Illustrated with: at the Art Musings show *Drawn & Quartered*; *Safety belt for the census bureau*, acrylic on canvas, 70" x 47", 2011 [a 2011 work placed in the 2007 to 2008 chapter]; *Colossal Sleeper*, acrylic on canvas, 60" x 60", 2008; *The dark's her bone*, acrylic on canvas, 60" x 60", 2008.

## 2009 to 2010: A New Hope and the American Chapter

In early 2009, inspired by Barack Obama's HOPE campaign, Raghava moved with Netra, their four-month-old baby, and their dog to New York City. It was a leap of faith, an artistic reset.

While Netra attended Columbia University, Raghava dedicated himself to caring for their child while simultaneously diving into the New York art scene. He exhibited his series on dysfunctional families at the Giacobetti Paul Gallery and Aicon Gallery in NYC and the Skotia Gallery in New Mexico. His work had evolved: more reflective, deeply personal, capturing the anxieties and absurdities of family life.

During this time, Raghava missed India deeply, immersing himself in the works of authors Ramachandra Guha and Shashi Tharoor. This sparked a series exploring migration, the experience of living in America, and letting go of his Indian roots, while also celebrating India's plurality. Viewing India through a foreign lens, he appreciated its nature as an "impossible democracy" and created a patriotic children's book that he later, in 2011, adapted to the iPad; when shaken, it offered Pakistan's perspective, or the British perspective.

Then came an invitation that would change everything. In 2010, Raghava was invited to give a full-length TED Talk at the prestigious TED Conference in Long Beach, California. He was among the first Indians to take the TED stage. His talk, "Five Lives of an Artist", was a revelation. He took the audience through his transformations, demonstrating how art, life, and reinvention were inseparable. The talk received standing ovations and captured the attention of global visionaries, Bill Gates, Vinod Khosla, and Jeff Bezos among them. TED didn't just give Raghava an audience; it validated his belief that art could shape minds, shift perspectives, and engineer empathy.

The TED platform gave him access to influential circles. It led to conversations with Silicon Valley pioneers, partnerships with technology companies, and collaborations with art institutions worldwide. His work was no longer confined to galleries; it was entering the digital, philosophical, and technological spaces.

## 2010 to 2017: A Cultural Phenomenon

The success of his TED Talk launched a decade of global influence and multidisciplinary projects. Over the next seven years, Raghava delivered five TED Talks, each evolving his ideas about perspective, empathy, and art's role in the digital age. He was now not just an artist but an intellectual provocateur, sought after by corporations, media, and cultural institutions. His talks and presentations took him to Google, CNN, BBC, The New York Times, WIRED UK, The United Nations, The National Geographic Society, Intel, Infosys, BOMA Paris, Cannes Lions Festival, and Life is Beautiful Festival. His insights were published by Big Think, TED MED and Deutsche Welle, and he advised governments like Israel and Qatar on creativity, innovation, and the future of technology in the arts.

His work during these years expanded beyond traditional art spaces. He created 50-foot live paintings for public installations. He developed interactive books like *Pop-it*, which won the Kirkus Book Award and was showcased at The Best of Singapore Art Fair.

In 2010, he co-founded and chaired The INK Fellowship, a prestigious multidisciplinary global fellowship supporting creative entrepreneurs. Through INK, he mentored some of the most promising minds in art, technology, and social change, infusing his philosophy of constant reinvention into their work.

Then, in 2013, another major milestone: Raghava was inducted into the National Geographic Society as an Explorer, one of the few artists to receive this honor. This recognition was a testament to his boundary-breaking approach to creativity, where science, technology, and human expression coexisted seamlessly.

During this period, Netflix also spotlighted him in an episode of *Creative Indians* (Season 2, Episode 10), a documentary series that showcased his wide-ranging influence across disciplines. His ability to narrate his life's artistic evolution captivated a whole new generation of collectors and enthusiasts.

His creative collaborations extended into music: he created album art for icons like Erykah Badu and Paul Simon. As creator-in-residence at Viacom, he premiered *Must#*, a groundbreaking Visual Empathy Algorithm, encouraging viewers to transcend personal tastes and embrace new perspectives. Art, for Raghava, was never a static experience; it was an active dialogue with the world.

In 2013, Raghava was invited as the opening panelist at the launch of Indiaspora, a platform bringing together the world's most influential Indian minds in America. It was there that he met Viswa Subbaraman, a visionary opera conductor, sparking a collaboration that would merge classical music, painting, and cutting-edge technology.

Raghava's paintings became the visual soul of a bold new theatrical staging of Beethoven's opera *Fidelio*, where he served as both art director and artist. But he didn't stop at set design. He integrated brainwave technology, connecting sensors to Subbaraman's head, allowing his neural activity to control elements of the stage lighting and interactive art installation. The result was a mesmerizing fusion of opera and live generative art, a performance that redefined the boundaries between classical music and contemporary artistic expression. The Skylight Theatre Production in Milwaukee received rave reviews, praised for its innovative use of technology, immersive visuals, and the seamless dialogue between painting, music, and neuroscience.

## 2015 to 2017: AI, Technology and the Reinvention of the Artist

As his career surged, Raghava's fascination with technology deepened. By 2015, he was collaborating with Artmatr, an offshoot of the MIT Media Lab, where he worked with a team that made SOZO, an innovative painting machine that merged human intuition with mechanical precision. This was not just about automation; it was about asking what happens when machines enter the creative process.

His pioneering work with AI caught the attention of the art world, earning him the prestigious Eric Fischl Residency in the Hamptons, USA. His fascination with neural networks, fractals, and data compression began influencing his aesthetic. He saw parallels between the organic chaos of human creativity and the structured logic of systems, technology and AI.

In 2017, Raghava was invited to be the artistic producer for *Shanti Samsara*, an ambitious environmental consciousness album created by Grammy-winning composer Ricky Kej, featuring over 200 musicians, including more than 60 Grammy Award winners [body text spells the composer "Rickey Kej", caption "Ricky Kej"]. The album was a global call to action for climate change, blending diverse musical traditions with a powerful message of sustainability.

*Shanti Samsara* was launched at COP21 in Paris by Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi alongside French Prime Minister François Hollande [Hollande was President of France; the caption dates the Paris Climate Summit unveiling to 2015, while the body text says the invitation came in 2017]. The project stood at the intersection of art, activism, and diplomacy, using music as a force for ecological awareness on the world stage.

In 2017, Raghava was invited by Jeff Bezos to speak, curate, and conduct an art workshop at an exclusive gathering of the world's most influential creative minds. Attendees included Tom Hanks, Michael J. Fox, Oprah Winfrey, George R.R. Martin, Neil Gaiman, Jamie Lee Curtis, Lin-Manuel Miranda, Billie Jean King, Tom Ford, and Tory Burch. It was clear that Raghava's reach was now global, stretching far beyond the traditional art world into philosophy, futurism, and technological thought leadership.

## 2018 to 2024: The Digital Renaissance and AI Art Revolution

By 2018, Raghava co-founded 64/1, an art collective with his brother, Harvard economist Dr. Karthik Kalyanaraman. Their goal was radical: to erase the boundaries between art, criticism, and education, challenging what art could be and how it should be experienced.

With an Indo-futuristic lens, the collective envisions a global future, drawing on India's rich cultural heritage. 64/1's first major project, "Transcendence for the Sirioscene," published in 2018 in Critical Collective, offered a novel perspective on art history and human identity in the future.

Through 64/1, Raghava co-curated the world's first AI-driven art exhibition at Nature Morte, launching the careers of today's leading AI artists like Memo Akten, Jake Elwes, Mario Klingemann, Anna Ridler, and Tom White. This show reshaped how India viewed new media art and positioned Raghava and Karthik at the forefront of the AI-art movement.

"Future Bets" by 64/1 in 2019 was an interactive performance installation that encouraged long-term vision among traders, involving writers, multimedia artists, economists, and game theorists.

In 2019, Raghava traveled to Bali to visit his friend, collaborator, and former mentee Shilo Shiv Suleman. What began as a reunion turned into a deep artistic awakening as he immersed himself in Bali's ancestral sculpting traditions. Captivated by the island's rich history of woodcarving, he trained under master sculptor Aditya Budyasa, making his debut into wooden sculpture. This experience marked a new dimension in his practice, one that blended ancient craftsmanship with his own contemporary artistic language.

For India's premier art magazine Marg's 75th anniversary in 2020, 64/1 explored technological change and its impact on our relationship with the biosphere.

In 2023, 64/1 engaged students in the "Meaning Hackathon," guiding them to create mobile art installations, democratizing contemporary aesthetics for a global future. This collective voice from India seeks to inspire and shape the global art scene.

As the world shut down in 2020 due to COVID-19, Raghava pivoted once again. He created *Eye Candy*, a performance-art piece on Instagram in collaboration with Under 25 Studios, that reached new audiences beyond the conventional art world. By blending painting, monologue, and self-help into a dynamic digital form, he proved that even in isolation, art could remain interactive and communal.

64/1's collaborative AI artwork, the *Strange Genders* series, co-created with Harshit Agrawal, was exhibited at Emami Art Foundation's *Exostential* exhibition and longlisted for the prestigious Lumen Prize, cementing their place as pioneers of AI-driven, digital-first art. The series explored the fluidity of gender by training AI on line drawings of a man and a woman, then asking it to generate every possible in-between state. The AI, unrestricted by human biases, created unexpected forms that challenged traditional binaries and invited viewers to reconsider identity through the lens of computation. This project was exhibited not only at Emami Art but also at Nvidia's AI Art Gallery in 2021, gaining recognition for its bold reimagination of gender beyond social constructs.

### The Orgasm Project

In 2021, Raghava KK launched *The Orgasm Project*, an audacious experiment at the intersection of neuroscience, AI, and robotic painting. Collaborating with neuroscientist Abhijeet Satani [caption: "Abhijit Satani"], he used EEG technology to record his brainwaves during orgasm, capturing the neurological patterns of an intensely personal experience. These brainwave signals were then processed by AI scientist Harshit Agrawal, who transformed the raw data into a 3D digital model, generating both animated visuals and still images that mapped the activity of the brain at climax.

The final phase of the project brought the digital into the physical world. Partnering with Artmatr, experts in robotic painting technology, Raghava converted the AI-generated visualizations into a large-scale oil painting (74" x 74"), created with a robotic arm programmed to translate brainwave data into expressive brushstrokes. This process not only blurred the boundaries between the human and the machine, the intimate and the public, but also raised profound ethical questions about the commodification of human experiences. The project debuted at Sotheby's x Burning Man, where it sold for $94,500, igniting global conversations about the future of art, technology, and the monetization of intimacy.

### The Edges Series

His work continued to evolve. In 2022, he showcased *The Edge Series* at Hub India, Artissima (Milan), represented by Nature Morte. The *Edges* series emerged from a dialogue between Raghava KK and his brother, art critic Dr. Karthik Kalyanaraman. Karthik would pose a philosophical question, and after three days of contemplation, Raghava would translate his response onto canvas. Over several months, he developed a new visual language where the edges of the canvas became sites of tension and transformation. Thick impasto, textured scars, and shifting velocities of paint evoked the body's fragility and the impossibility of containment. Some works played with unconventional mounting, near the ground, parallel to the floor, or as luminous installations, while others explored the presence of absence through negative marks and layered compositions. The *Edges* series was more than an artistic evolution; it was a living conversation, a meditation on perception, form, and erasure.

### Impossible Bouquet

In 2023, *Impossible Bouquet* debuted as the opening exhibition at the India Art Fair, exploring AI's role in digital image-making. Raghava KK trained an AI on his brushstrokes and *Impossible Bouquet* style, allowing it to generate new compositions. But this wasn't a one-way process. Raghava painted in the AI's style, mirroring its interpretation of his strokes, creating a feedback loop where both artist and machine learned from each other.

The AI introduced unexpected choices in composition, color, and form, challenging Raghava's instincts and expanding his artistic vocabulary. The final artworks, archival prints layered with his hand-painted additions, embodied a fusion of human intuition and machine logic. More than an exhibition, *Impossible Bouquet* became a dialogue between man and machine, blurring the lines of authorship and creativity.

### Where the Wind Tastes of Forgotten Songs

Raghava KK created a site-specific immersive installation for the Royal Enfield Art Festival, inspired by the transformative power of the Himalayas. Titled "Where The Wind Tastes of Forgotten Songs," the work blended painting, drawing, animation, AI, digital painting, sound design, projection mapping, and interactive art to evoke the duality of the Himalayas: their imposing vastness and intimate whispers, raw power and fragile beauty. The installation invited viewers to experience the Himalayan winds as storytellers, carrying the scents of sacred forests, ancient chants, and murmurs of history, creating a portal into a world of wonder, transformation, and harmony. It was shown at the Travancore Palace, New Delhi, for the Royal Enfield Social Mission [source: "Travencore"].

He is not just documenting the evolution of art; he is leading it, breaking down the barriers between artist, machine, and audience. Whether through AI, digital storytelling, or radical reinventions, Raghava remains a provocateur, a visionary, and above all, an artist whose journey is far from over.
