## Transcendence for the Siri-ocene, or an Art for the Future (2018)

By 64/1 (Karthik Kalyanaraman and Raghava KK) [source has an unmatched closing parenthesis after the authors' names]

Published in South Asia's top academic art criticism venue, this was a utopian thesis about what transcendence in art can mean for the future. Along the way it developed an original lens with which to view all of art history: as movements partially determined by our changing notions of who we are as humans.

Link to essay: https://docs.google.com/document/d/1bWb6tCgih7lItibrSD1UiblwSe1jvl25QcK6rOeNHs/edit?usp=sharing

The full essay is reproduced in the book (27 numbered pages). Its text follows. Footnotes are given as bracketed numbers and collected under Notes at the end of the essay.

### Roadmap

The first part of the essay, written as a polemic, takes issue with the melancholia and preoccupation with the past prevalent in art theory after the end of postmodernism. While these attitudes are also common in the Global South, the notion of the 'end of History' which symbolizes this attitude, seems peculiarly Eurocentric from our perspective as Westernized Indians. We finds [sic] our crux of disagreement with the adoption, after Nietzsche and Deleuze, of a wholesale politico-aesthetics of immanence: of notions of situatedness, critical revisions of the past and so on. When everything is reduced to a debate between biology and culture, the notion of the future as possibility gets overshadowed. This might be tolerable in times of technological and economic stasis, but the article argues that we, today, are in a position where two large specters have occupied all imagination of our future: the increasing depth of automation as symbolized by the rise of artificial intelligence and increasing fears of ecological collapse. In such times, rather than asking why we are who we are, it might be more fruitful to ask what can we be to keep faith with our future selves.

The second theoretical part of the essay takes on the notion of transcendence, motivated by an ontology where the heart of being is seen as unsettled. We cannot turn the wheel back to old notions of transcendence which rely on a foundational, absolute notion like God or Truth. Similarly the Kantian sublime cannot be revived unchanged after an immanentist geneological [sic] critique. From a transcultural perspective that borrows insights from the early Jena Romantics, from Tantric Saivism and from Jainism, we propose a new notion, affective [1] rather than cognitive, of the sublime and the transcendent. This provides an alternative to recent theories of the contemporary sublime like Gilbert-Rolfe's where the sublime object is Technology. The object of the new experience of the sublime is relational and radically incomplete. It is created by the participation of the subject, while it simultaneously constitutes the subject through that participation. The notion of the Future symbolizes perfectly this new object of the sublime experience.

The last part of the essay is written partly as summary of recent trends and partly as a manifesto, a hope we have for an art of the future. We see a role for the artist in providing us anew, not unlike the great Russian filmmakers of the 1920s, with creative visions of our future selves. An engagement that does not accept technological change passively but quickens the social imaginary of the viewer. The future artist will be working with a model of the self, where the rational, the affective and the machine-like are all intertwined. She will have to engage with art becoming more participatory, with our experience of reality becoming more deeply mediated by our technology and with the rise of machines that might be able to out-compete her in most of her cognitive and pattern-recognitions tasks, including the making of an art of a certain kind. We end with a proposal for a new aesthetics of the transcendent form, one we call Relational. Shelley dreamed of a world where artists were the legislators of humankind. This sounds terribly far-fetched now. But it is equally dispiriting to have artists reduce themselves to irrelevance in a rapidly changing world because of their obsessions with situatedness, their identity and the past.

### Polemic: Of ends and beginnings

History, it seems, has ended, and so has Art. Ever since Fukuyama popularized the idea, once limited to Hegelian-Marxian circles, in 1992, it has been bandied about so much that it seems to provoke not the slightest recoil, at least in academia and pseudo-academia. Even the wonderful Hal Foster, one of the most perceptive Art critics of our times, after paying due deference to this notion, in an explicit attempt to formulate futures for Art, cannot "deny that our condition is one largely of aftermath" (Foster 2003: 125). But who is the 'our' he refers to? What does the 'aftermath' entail?

The End of History. Even if one recognizes that what is at stake is a sense of dialectical possibility in history, which certainly seems depleted after the implosion of the last pragmatic alternative in 1989 to the seeming inevitability of late-capitalist liberal democracy [2], the phrase still strikes the Indian ear as absurd and Eurocentric. Here we are in the seeming springtime of our possibilities and history has ended?! Indeed, why should dialectics and Hegelian have a monopoly on our understand [sic] of the currents of History? Why, if Braudel's model was our guide, and the central motor of world history [3] was but the cultural exuberance from centers of intense economic activity, it might strike one that History has indeed gone on, only elsewhere. Or consider Vico's [4] cyclical theories for that matter, or cyclical Kalacakra theories, in which quite sensibly History is always ending and always beginning. Or take the postmodern retreat from objectivity and architectonics and the insistence that there never was a History, only a morass of histories that are already always 'relativist perspectives'. [5]

The End of History? Perhaps one shouldn't be so sure that late-capitalist liberal democracy is the only option we are left with. And this is not merely a matter of the Gramscian 'pessimism of intellect and optimism of will' [6]. Witness the surprising rise of new fascisms across the Western world. History does have a habit of delivering surprises to its self-elected prophets like Fukuyama, who claimed that liberal democracy was the 'final form of human government'.

And presumably the End of Art is a mere superstructural emanation from the End of History. As narrated by the Hegelian schools, Art, which was once, during the Romantic Age, the very key to its epoch, has been demoted by the evolution of Spirit, to handmaiden status [7] as a minor club activity, to not even keeping up with the times in these times: Art has ended. But did Art in this sense ever begin? Was Art ever the key to any Age, when it barely managed to express the lifeworld of its mostly elite producers (mostly producing under the whims of a super-elite patronage in most pre-Modern Ages), let alone the masses? Or was this simply the pipe-dream of the Romantics themselves or the usual Hegelian esprit de l'escalier of finding that pattern which would lead to the putative reign of (Hegelian) Philosophy?

Of course there is that other, more pragmatic, end of Art: the 'anything goes' aesthetics of the frame, the tin can tied to the postmodern puppy born of Duchampian modernism. Put it in a museum, or in the hands of a theorist for theorists like Arthur Danto, and voilà, black paint with a theory stapled on is now Art. Art, a mere name now for the hermeneutic drive, is everywhere: so nowhere. The artist is dead, long live the art critic. But this notion too has rather inevitably run out of steam, partly as a mere logical consequence of the ease of creating made-to-order theoretical props to support practically anything, and partly due to the concrete economic requirements of the art ecosystem, which can't sell all that can be framed. So then what comes in the wake of Art?

In any case, what all these Ends have been implicated in, is a sense that the only way forward is a "making-do with what comes after" (Foster 2003: 129). An immanent aesthetics of shoring fragments against one's ruin, or more uncharitably, of making one's melancholia and wallowing in it. A poetics with an implied hollowing out of any politics, of a situation where the future, as an active site of imagination and struggle, is already a dry, emptied foreclosed thing.

Let us for a moment look more closely at one of the most thoughtful recent proposals for a future for Art after Art: Hal Foster's closing chapter to his book Design and Crime, in which he tries to rally flagging art-historical spirits and find precisely, to use Wallace Stevens' formulation [source misspells the name as "Wzallace"], "what will suffice" for this aftermath.

After trenchantly critiquing the pragmatic thinness of both the neo avant-garde forming itself in relation to the performative/process/conceptual art praxes of the 1960s, and the postmodern with its negations (of modernist monumentality, painterliness, and plastic values), which dispersed itself in orgies of apophasis, or as he puts it in "compounds without much tension" (2003: 127), Foster proposes four thematic nuclei of and for the art of living on, which turns out to be an art of memory: the traumatic, the spectral, the nonsynchronous and the incongruent.

Of these, the first two are quite obviously backward-facing: Meaning lies in the past and its compulsive repetition: the traumatic, perhaps most obvious in Art attempting to come to terms with the Holocaust, is Art trying to face the trauma of History, and in so, witnessing a creation of meaning for the present; the spectral, evoking the ghostly persistence of the lost past, is Art as Hamlet Sr., a melancholy revenant that yet is machinery for the plot of the present. And it turns out the nonsynchronous is equally haunted by the past, though not obvious right from the outset, it is a strategy of making new media "out of the remnants of old forms" (2003: 137). Foster, in 2002, gave as an example William Kentridge's projected satirical drawings; and the title of one of the latter's works from 2007 says it all: "What will come (has already come)". And as for the last category, the incongruent, precisely what distinguishes it from postmodern conflations, negations, and conflations of negations of modernist practices is entirely unobvious: the works cited seem yet more examples of postmodern reframings, but less concerned with pure pastiche and more socially situated, which perhaps then constitutes yet another mode of an Imitation of (past) Life.

This condition, of being a dream of a shadow projected from the past, is ultimately the best Foster seems to hope for from the art of the future.

Now let's pause, lift up our heads from our books, walk out of the hallowed groves of Academe, and look at two titanic utopian spirits, which willy-nilly have entirely possessed all imagination of futurity.

The Advent of the Machines. Between 1995 and 2002, a mere 7 years, as Martin Ford documents (2015), China lost about 15% of its manufacturing workforce, close to 16 million jobs, due to advancing automation. McKinsey estimates (2017: 5), in what is probably a conservative guess, that 51% of job-related activities in the U.S. now can be automated. And these aren't just repetitive tasks: in 2000, Goldman Sachs in New York employed 600 equities traders: in 2017 there were 2 humans and a few smart trading algorithms, supported by 200 computer programmers; some of these "algorithms ... designed to emulate as closely as possible what a human would do" (Nanette Byrnes, 2017). This is all of course the very beginning of an accelerating trend, likely to cause cataclysms in the structure of the labor market, in both blue and white collar labor markets, and in the structure of civil society: the digitalization of labor and the rise of artificial intelligence. On the other hand, this is also cause for a great deal of optimism about technological singularities and post-human postlabor futures among the Silicon Valley futurologists like Ray Kurzweil. However, many observers outside the tech industry seem to think we might be delivering ourselves up to our machines, into a future Utopia for elite posthumans, and mass disenfranchisement for those who have had the misfortune to have remained all-too-human. And so, is this really the End of History? With such a radically strange future ahead, should we not say, History is just beginning?

The Anthropocene apocalypse. With each passing year, concerns about global climate change, predictions of its effects on the future of the world, become more dire. And the world union of nations seems utterly unable to take any steps towards reducing emissions. Recent bestselling popular science accounts, either envision a radically different future with a drastically reduced human population (e.g. Oreskes and Conway's The Collapse of Western Civilization (2014)), or counsel a stoic acceptance of the end not of our individual adventure but of our end as a species (Roy Scranton's Learning to die in the Anthropocene (2015)). History might not be ending, only making way in a phase-shift for a strange new place, a new age perhaps without us, a utopia for what truly will come after us, the post human.

It is our life, under the shadow of these two ambiguous spirits, that we call the digital Anthropocene, or the Siri-ocene. It is future as both dissolution and new dawn; pralaya and srishti.

And Art? Where is art in the midst of all this talk about aftermaths? What happens to Foster's categories with the advent of artificial intelligence? Consider: the main motivation for Foster's proposals is to create an art that "through formal transformation that is also social engagement.... helps to restore a mnemonic dimension to contemporary art." [8] (2003: 130) In the wake of postmodernism, which for him has not really been 'thick' enough to form the soil for the rise of a new tradition, Foster hopes that a transformed recuperation of the past can form the nucleus for the development of new art practices, in other words, of a new tradition.

This valorization of memory in the service of an immanent social critique is not unique to Foster. Art theory post-Deleuze has been one unending paean to the immanent, in India and elsewhere. Take for instance Geeta Kapur's thoughtful essay for the 2012 Kochi-Muziris Biennale which starts with the description of an artwork (Rigo 23's installations which included the tribute to black migrants workers of the Portuguese past, the kappiris) that seems designed to Foster's specification of 'haunting art', and goes on to end with praise of the Biennale's critical reworking of the past, of the 'palimpsest of residues' and of 'replenish[ing] the past-present continuum'. The animating idea behind the praise is the importance of 'critical contemporaneity' in supposedly 'dystopic times'. Neither assumption, that "artists are always situated, but always liminal" (Kapur 2013: 176) or that the present is dystopic (or as for Foster, is the end of dialectical possibility), is uncommon in the academic Left. The role acceded to Art then is pure immanentist negativity, a 'critical contemporaneity', a skeptical undercutting of "hegemonic tendencies in all [sic] cultural formations."

The notion that we already live in a dystopia needs of course to be confronted, or at least tempered, with a few reality-checks. For example, by taking into consideration data on the massive reduction in global absolute and relative poverty levels (since 1990, extreme poverty has been declining by 47 million every year, see Roser and Ortiz-Espina, 2018), better public health outcomes, reduction in violence and increase in education in the near past. And as Marc Augé usefully reminds us: "Being contemporary means concentrating on those things in the present that sketch something of the future.... Contemporaneity isn't to be reduced to passing events and is conjugated in the future perfect." [9] (2015: 66-7)

But setting such merely empirical considerations aside, and acknowledging that a certain criticality may be germane to Art, the crucial problem for us lies in the unruffled faith in 'situatedness' and immanence, in what Frédéric Neyrat calls "saturated immanence". [10] That is, in a post-Deleuzian, post-postmodern condition, in which everything is so obsessively its history, there is no otherness, no ontological or temporal threshold for emancipation, psychological or social: a condition indeed of An End....

And Foster's categories? How simple it would be to design a neural network, let's call it Funes 2.0, which having seen and memorized images of every known artwork available to humankind, is the very repository of the Unconscious of art-history! An algorithm which would be able to produce via permutation and selection from different semantic fields, haunted images that mnemonically recuperate images of history, of art-history, of 'remnants of old forms' like the film, the comic strip and so on. Can we resist finding meaning in these automatic metonymies?

Indeed the first baby steps have already been taken in artist Memo Akten's project 'Learning to Dream'. His neural network, which has studied all the images on Google's Art database, is able to produce other original images, hallucinations from a possible alternative history of painting. The remarkable fact is that each one of the paintings produced by the algorithm, while distinct, also seem to bear clear art-historical allusions, say to Bosch, or a 17th century Dutch landscape.

[Illustration: From Learning to Dream, Memo Akten]

And there's the rub: it is only so far as we believe the sources of our aesthetic creativity are mysteries, so far as we believe we are opaque to ourselves, that we can continue to believe we cannot create machine-artists. An immanent poetics of being and of time, a focus on what we already are in our conscious selves, and have been as historical creatures, whether in the form of an identity politics or merely an exclusive insistence on memory, is a poetics ripe for takeover by the machine. History and memory, inasmuch as they are mere information, are ready for encroachment by the digital. And given our inability to quench our interpretive drive, will an insistence that history is not information but hermeneutics, save us from finding in the very quantity, diversity, and profound connection to art-history of the art produced by Funes 2.0, the apotheosis of the art of Mnemosyne?

Is this then the exhausted Endgame for Art, a confinement to a stance of critical negativity to supposedly hegemonic ideology, all the while being part of yet another (definitely hegemonic) market of signs and of commodities? Should artists abandon optimism, vision and architecture to the trigger-happy technocrat and confine themselves to a termite politics of remembering and undermining?

While the past and memory may well be what will suffice for Ends, they cannot serve Beginnings. It is in the future, which is never information but imagination, and in the transcendent, which is restored otherness, which is everything we have not been, that we might find for art a program tough enough to venture into reality; reality not as a dead given but as a live dialectic that manufactures us while being manufactured by us. And what follows is our little attempt at dreaming of a new role for the artist of the future. Our theory is animated by our conviction that History, in a certain sense, is just dawning. For after all, as Emerson, who divided all politics into a clash of the party of Hope and the party of Memory, astutely observed "We are reformers in spring and summer; in autumn and winter, we stand by the old; reformers in the morning, conservers at night" (Emerson 1841).

### Theory: A version of transcendence

Talk of transcendence has a bad rep. If not considered defunct because of its connection with the Judeo-Christian notion of God, it is distrusted for its post-Enlightenment associations with suspect universal notions of the Absolute, the Unconditioned and so on. It is not the perfect term for what we want to talk about, which is a kind of ontological restlessness, a curiosity about boundaries and liminality, a kind of drive to not dissolve the self in nothingness, but to disturb its sedimentation, to change its conditions. If the allied notion of the Sublime (a species concept which we will happily run together for our purposes with the genus concept of transcendence) did not carry so much Kantian freight, we might have resorted to it instead. We will try instead to redefine transcendence for our purposes, in effect for the digital Anthropocene, or the Siri-ocene, as we call it.

The Kantian idea of the sublime, refers to that, which starting from the realm of nature leads out of the sensuous to consciousness of our freedom and, therein, our own infinity and autonomy. Or as Kant puts it, we are led by the sublime to find in our own faculty of reason another, nonsensible standard, which has that very infinity under itself as a unit against which everything in nature is small, and thus [to find] in our own mind a superiority over nature itself even in its immeasurability (2000: 145).

Such a sublime is an amalgam of the twin concepts of infinity and autonomy [11], associated with the mathematical and the dynamic sublime respectively. This sublime finds its end in Reason's natural drive towards the unconditioned. Thus crucially, the Kantian sublime is that which begins in Art and the representable, and leads us beyond nature and aesthetic representability. Hegel's absolute idealism, would remove the critical limits imposed by Kant (because even though Reason drives towards the unconditioned, it can never by definition reach the thing-in-itself). He would claim Art is a necessary though mediate stage in the attainment of the Absolute, (his own) philosophy taking the last steps into the Absolute's self-apprehension.

It is this sublime, and variations of it (developed most prominently by Lyotard and more recently Jeremy Gilbert-Rolfe [12]), that have played a large role in art theory and practice. The problem of accepting this notion of the transcendent, as that which leads us out of the finite and the conditioned (peras, to use Aristotelian terminology) into the infinite and the unconditioned (apeiron), now becomes apparent. It is precisely such (a Marxian would say, reified) concepts, which promise a view from nowhere, and promise to take us beyond the merely natural, that fall prey to Nietzsche's corrosive genealogical critique. And since the Nietzschean death of God, and after the work of post-Nietzschean thinkers like Derrida and Deleuze, the transcendent and the sublime have been assumed to be superseded, yielding pride of place to immanence. To such a degree that today even to bring up the notion of immanence seems [13] unnecessary, so absolutized has it become: things are obviously what they are, and any talk of reaching beyond one's nature is either delusion or deluding rhetoric. But the idea that this pure transcendence and pure immanence are the only available ontologies, that this sharp dichotomy is exhaustive of the field of being, is a mistake.

And it is a mistake sustained by treating the incredible intellectual ferment between Kant and Hegel as a gap. We can borrow a page from that brief floruit (1795-1800) of the Jena Romantics and their attempt to harmonize reason and nature [14] [source prints "re son"], or as Hölderlin would conceptualize it, the Christian and the Pagan, in the aesthetic object. For these Frühromantiker, like the Schlegels, Hölderlin and Novalis, the sublime and the transcendent were never epistemological concepts, as they partly were for Kant and would wholly become in the hands of Hegel and other idealists, but purely aesthetic, that is, partly affective [15], concepts. As Novalis puts it, "we look everywhere for the Unconditional Absolute, and all we find are the conditions." (203: 2003). Or as Gorodeisky explains: While the idealists [Schelling, Fichte, Hegel] took the Absolute to be transparent to the human mind, conceptually representable and inferentially related to other items of knowledge, the romantics regarded it as (1) ungraspable by concepts (i.e. as nondiscursive) and (2) as nonfoundational [16] (2016). Thus Schlegel could say with superb derision: "There are writers who drink the absolute like water; and books in which even the dogs refer to the infinite." (1991: 7).

In short: pure transcendence is unattainable, a soul without body, a ghost; pure immanence is non-existent, an idealization, given that all things as images in the human mind move beyond themselves [17]. If the former is a senile dream of wanting to be all spirit and yet apprehend material existence, the latter is mad sparagmos, a desire to delude oneself into seeing life in the parts of a living body dismembered.

So for us: the transcendent as drive, as felt experience of motion out of the limits of a situated self; instead of an ontology of self and other, for us empirical being itself is constituted by an otherness at its heart, that drives it outside its constantly congealing limits. The transcendent then as a drive towards a truer vision of the self (once known as the rational soul or reason) linked to a supra-individual object (once known as God or the Absolute). As for the Jena Romantics, our notion of the transcendent is primarily an experiential, non-discursive notion; we, like them, want a notion of the object of the transcendent experience that is not some clearly existing foundational thing-in-itself but is, instead, a responsive movement to the call of its object; that is, this object is not some self-established, autopoetic, autoerotic Absolute (like the Judeo-Christian 'god' or the thing-in-itself of Kant or even ultimately, in our opinion, the 'substance' of Spinozists) but a nonfoundational, relational, desirous thing. Now, it falls upon us to inject some content into these constraints: What experience? What is its object? Which truer self?

(A quick digression: In Kashmir Saivite thought, for instance Abhinavagupta's Santarasa aesthetics (Masson et al., 1969), the idea of a transcendent 1) never severs itself from the sensuous (the classic image of the embrace of Siva and Parvati in which the former recognizes himself in the mirror held by the latter but remains in the embrace, unlike say Kant's sublime where self-recognition leads altogether away from materiality, the sensuous, and the aesthetic); and 2) stems from the fundamental structure of being. This bears some resemblance to our proposal. Abhinavagupta's concern seems to have been to harmonize his religious belief as a Tantric saivite with his evident delight in secular drama; this is not our concern. But comparisons of our notion with his will be useful and require further study.)

For Kant, the experience of the sublime led to an realization [sic] of the superiority of the mind's powers even over the awesomeness of Nature; this revelation was important, its affective content less so. Our priorities are reversed: we are interested in precisely this sensuous experience, that aspect of the encounter with art that eludes (but doesn't, cannot, banish) discursive thought. To characterize the experience, we borrow a phrase from the profound work of the prominent theorist of the religious experience, Rudolf Otto: mysterium tremendum et fascinans. He uses this formulation to characterize [18] what is distinctive in religious experience, as opposed to religious thought or dogma. However what he calls 'the Numen', his word for the ground and telos of religious feeling, performs no theoretical heavylifting, if one ignores the tendentious Christian apologetics in the second less-impressive half of the work. We see no reason for entities like 'God', 'The Dao', 'Dhamma', 'Brahman' etc. in this theory; the work of art will do.

Mysterium tremendum et fascinans. A feeling of something mysterious that is both awe-inspiring and attractively fascinating. Mysterious, because the object of experience of the artwork is caught up in a dialectic of concealment and revelation and of promise, that is, a fulfillment deferred in the elicitation and evasion of the restless will to interpretation. Nothing that stays a mystery can be a cognitively masterable Absolute. Neither can it be perfectly self-contained; it is its very incompletion that calls forth and constitutes the makeshift subject of experience. The experience is ambivalent, essential for any dialectical conception of reality: experienced both as terror and wonder, as awe and fascination, as a force that beckons and a force that repels, a force which disturbs the settled self out of its sedimentations, its empirical personality, its socio-historical identity, its 'nature'.

Variously troped throughout intellectual history as brokenness (Plato's Aristophanes in Symposium), separation (of siva and sakti in Tantric Saivism), love (of Dante and the stilnovisti and perhaps of the later troubadours), drive (towards union/pleasure and inorganicity/death in Freud), fallenness (of the divine sparks in the Lurianic tradition), freedom (Kant), the other-in-the-same (Levinas), and so on and on, there is a restlessness at the heart of Being. Transcendence is merely this restlessness released in recognition and will. It is the counter-myth to the ideas of identity, perfectibility and self-subsistence; it is the reversal of hypostasis, the drive behind all true politics.

(Before we move on, a few words about the connections of our theory with recent revisionings of the sublime. Our theory bears little connection to Lyotard's Holocaust-haunted misprision of Kant: while for Kant the sublime is that which leads out of representation into practical reason, for Lyotard the sublime is a (paradoxically) eloquent representational witness to the gulf between the image and the thing, between word and object, attesting to the failure of the mimetic ambition. [19] The theory however does bear comparison to Jeremy Gilbert-Rolfe's revaluation of beauty in his wonderful Beauty and the Contemporary Sublime (1999). Gilbert-Rolfe's main concerns are twofold. 1) He wants to reverse or at least neutralize the traditional valuation of beauty as somehow inferior to the sublime. He does this by equating beauty to its resistance to discursive appropriation [20]; beauty for him is its irresponsibility and its frivolity (inflected with Derrida's elaboration of the term). 2) He redefines the sublime in contemporary practice as that for which the image of the autonomous infinite is not found in Nature but in Technology. Gilbert-Rolfe differs from our theory in that: 1) his is still primarily a Kantian (i.e. epistemological) sublime, concerned with discursive Reason; ours is primarily an non-cognitive (i.e. aesthetic [21]) notion and therefore, as such, equally resistant to reason's imperialisms. The traditional distinction of beauty and the sublime is somewhat orthogonal to our distinction of transcendent/immanent. 2) As we shall see, technology is no standard bearer for our sublime; our 'transcendent' object is by definition mysterious, desirous and relational. However, there is a larger, deeper similarity of concern and motive, best expressed as a resistance to a 'hermeneutics of suspicion' and the 'disenfranchisement of art' at the expense of theory. A counter to the state of affairs that Gilbert-Rolfe describes: In the art world of the later rather than the earlier twentieth century, reason puts the art principal to work by forgetting it by except to the extent that it recalls it through its demystification (Gilbert-Rolfe 1999: 43).)

What can the incomplete relational object of transcendent experience be? Clearly any self-subsistent Absolute, source of the Kantian dynamical sublime, like the engulfing monumentality of Abstract Expressionist surfaces, will not fit the bill. Neither do Kantian notions of the mathematical sublime, the potential infinity of ratio, like the uncanny yet ego-dwarfing light installations of James Turrell or the all-too-present blankness that Gilbert-Rolfe finds in the ubiquitous electronic and plastic surfaces of today (173: 1997)]. [stray closing bracket in source] Nor is the glossy blankness of the techno-sublime, the black mirror of our mobile devices, mysterious. Though Gilbert-Rolfe associates the techno-sublime with inscrutability, and claims that 'the sublime may be seen to be necessarily inscrutable' (163: 1997), his (Kantian) sublime is not ours and inscrutability is not mysteriousness: the inscrutable is stolidly non-dialectical. Indeed technology, when unmodified by the totality of human purposes rather than just a blind logic of utility, seems more and more to be the opposite of what we want, a force that sweeps us onward according to its own developing logic rather than allow for engagement with human needs.

For us that object is the Future. But the future is inherently a dialectical conception: there is always a tension within it between that aspect of it, which is determined by the past and that other aspect of it, which is possibility, which is uncertain and therefore always bears promise. As Augé puts it, the future is both the fulfillment of a schematics linked to the past, a plot, and, more crucially, also an inauguration, the advent of the 'new', an act. Both Emersonian fate and, in counterbalance, Emersonian freedom; alive to constraint, but alert to possibility. In this the future is the ideal object of mystery, promising knowledge and frustrating it, terrifying in times of radical change, technological or otherwise, and in equal measure, fascinating for the social imaginary it quickens anew. This relational, incomplete entity, the future, is the object of our transcendent artwork, which through its interpellations calls us forth from our so-called selves.

Again, theological connections can be made to clarify our conception, theology and philosophy being, as far as we are concerned, merely different names for the same activity. The prior notion of the Absolute was reliant on the orthodox Judeo-Christian [22] notion of a autonomous God, on Aquinian aseity [23]; our notion of the Future as incomplete, calling on us for its creation, may be linked, on the collective or social level, to the Tikkun theology of the Hasidim, where the restoration of the broken splendor [24] of Godhead in exile, the Shekinah, both constitutes and is dependent on redemptive human action. On the individual/psychological level, our notion is similar to Ibn 'Arabi's notion of creative prayer [25], an activity to which one is summoned by the inner call of the divine, which is revealed in the mind as a desire for transcendence: this prayer is simultaneously created by and creative of that face of god uniquely revealed to the individual. This is no autoerotic [26] God, but one created by human desire just as much as this god creates the subject of this prayer in and through a Desire of his/her own. Substitute artwork for prayer and the future for the divine, and you have our notion at least on the purely individual level.

More connections can be made between our notion of the future and with the anekantavada of Jainism [27]. The transcendent object in Jaina thought was a Totality of connected, hanging, perspectival units (jivas, i.e. 'souls' except not changeless) rather than an Absolute, though impersonal, principle like the Brahman of the Vedantists; it [is, word missing in source] the obligation to mutual aid of these jivas ('parasparopagraho jivanam') that stitch them together into a totality. In any case, it is only an uninflected monotheism that has tormented us with both the absurd notion of pure transcendence and its equally absurd negation in the notion of pure immanence.

Our contention is that artists and art-theorists while focused on the melancholic aspect of future as fate, have dangerously left the notion of future as project to technologists, to scientists who regularly confuse the descriptive and the normative, and to that strange quasi-religious species of soothsayers known as 'futurists'. This is a terrible loss for life and for art, made possible largely due to art having adopted, wholesale, an uncritical aesthetics of immanence.

The Future, in this active sense of a set of possibilities, is the object of encounter with the transcendent artwork. As Augé says: "An encounter, contrary to heredity, heritage and destiny, is the ordeal of otherness (the reason why the word signifies empathy as well as confrontation) and an opening-up of time, adventure and freedom." (2015: 46)

### Interlude: A schematic history of the true self

Before we go on to what our dream of an art of the future may be, we still have to answer that third question: what is the true self? The true self, as opposed to our lived commonsensical network of beliefs and desires, the everyday self, has evolved and changed over the history of Modernity. Below is our schematic sketch of an ideal history of that self, of the relationship of that history to intellectual history, and of its relationship to art theories and movements. Given that every dispositif has to have a model of its subject (in both senses) implicit within it, it is not surprising that changes in the notion of what we believe we truly are, will be reflected in the ecology of the art world. Ours is a history divided into two halves: a chronicle of past changes and an extrapolated history of the future-in-the present.

Though the many pre-modern notions of the self are wildly different, one common feature they shared (as far as the authors know, at least in the Western, Indian and Islamic worlds) was the notion that the self was stratified, that it was composed of levels with increasing ontological weight. In the West, perhaps in germinal form already, with the notion of souls in Pythagoras and Empedocles, and in Aristotle's conception of the lower nutritive soul and the higher rational soul (or nous which all humans participate in), it finds somewhat definitive, at least influential, formulation with the Neoplatonists. Proclus, for instance [28], had the lowest vegetative soul (the body as quasi-mechanical process), the nonrational soul (the imagination and sense-perception), and the rational soul (intellection). For him, the aim of philosophy [29] was "to free oneself eventually from the lower psychic faculties, including the lower rational ones, in order to enjoy a state of pure contemplation" (Frede 2017) in touch with the source of all being, the One, which Christians were later to call 'God'. This model was resurrected in the Renaissance with Ficino and Cusanus, and was to prove important to all subsequent metaphysical thinking. And with the vital importance of Neoplatonist philosophy to speculative thought in the three Abrahamic faiths, it is not surprising to find it again in Islamic thought. For instance as Anne-Marie Schimmel documents, there is the tripartite model [30] of Hujwiri (~1000 C.E.), with the jasud (body), the nafs (mental self) and ruh (spirit), or the four-part self of Kharraz and Nuri (~900 C.E.). More surprisingly, but perhaps explicable as a feature of the phenomenological method rather than as historical exchange, are the levels in Hindu and Mahayana anthropology. The notion of a layered self, with each layer having different ontological/axiological weight, was feature of speculative thought from the time of the Taittiriya Upanishad (~500 B.C.E.) [source spells it "Taittìya"], with its 5 sheath model of the self [31]. And in Buddhism, the Yogacara school of Mahayana formalized the trikaya or the three-body doctrine [source spells it "trikìya"].

Two caveats: 1) We begin immediately post-Renaissance, and since ours is a schematic, 'ideal' history, a suggestive fable rooted in actual history, we use as our ideal-type (Weber's Idealtypus) a three-part model of the self. 2) Our history is a history of the modern self, and the history of modernity, though inflected differently in different cultural horizons, is largely a history of the erasure of locality. Hence, what follows will seem Eurocentric; this cannot be avoided: unfortunately, notwithstanding speculative nascent modernisms in India or China, modernity was largely Eurocentric thought (aided by Colonialist muscle).

At the beginning of the story, our self is defined with the help of three concepts, three levels, of increasing value, captured by the terms 'machine', 'animal' and 'human'. 'Machine' is to be understood as that part of ourselves that is pure mechanism, is isomorphic to the inorganic, follows set laws and principles, and is moved rather than moves. 'Animal', the totality of which we will sometimes call 'Nature', is that aspect of ourselves that is endowed with will, or sometimes, with just this thing called 'life'. By virtue of this possession, we are not a thing but an organism; we have intention and desire. The 'human' denoted different essences to different thinkers: typically associated with reason and ability to think in Western thought [32], with 'pure consciousness' in the Indian, and spirit (ruh) or the 'breath of God' in the Islamic. It was that most valuable part of ourselves which had a connection to Reality, to supraindividual Truth, which was variously called 'God' or 'the Absolute' or 'Substance' or even the 'Physical Laws' (when metaphors designed by mind to model observational regularities become invested with mind-independent existence).

Our story is a story of changes rung on this model. It is a story of three moments in intellectual history which allowed/caused the three terms to exchange values, disturbing the age-old, secure onto-axiological hierarchy. Of course changes in the structure of the self imply a change in the art produced by that self, or at least changes in the way those selves talk to themselves about the art they produce. Thus we will also, somewhat blithely, indicate connections between the changing self and art movements. This is not to be understood as temporal causation but as a logical emanation: the changing economy of the self restructures the discourse it produces, in this case, the theory of art. [33]

The first quake to unsettle the layers might well be the mythic origin of Modern philosophy: Descartes' publication in 1641 of his Meditations, in which his corrosive method of doubt shrunk all that was specifically human to the 'res cogitans' or the thinking thing, and left all the rest, 'res extensa', mere matter in its blind lumpishness. The 'animal' acquired the characteristics of the 'machine'. Nature, aside from the ghostly thinking substance in the human, lost the many souls and angels it had possessed since Aristotelian times; it became clockwork. Literally so, when Newton in his Principia (1687) reduced all motion, whether on earth or in heaven, to the matter of a single equation, his so called Second Law; he had already, of course, proposed that even light, famously created by divine fiat, was simply particles in motion. With this blow Nature started to lose its mystique, and mere representation of natural harmony in the arts, as in Poussin, its value. Reaction, consciously overt in the form of hostility to Newton in Blake, would set in in the form of the Romantic movement that stressed the imagination and inner self over representation of the putative harmonies of nature, what M.H. Abrams tropes as 'art as lamp' as against 'art as mirror'. And the movement towards interiority would only continue; the shock would leave repercussions that would last a long time: Impressionism, which was not about the perceived thing but the mode of perception in the mind; Fauvism, which detached color as an attribute of an object's appearance from color as expression of artist's attitude; Viennese Expressionism, which extended subjectively motivated 'distortions' to composition and line. And it continues even today in political cartoon, in the satirical drawing, and other art practices where violence is done to the object in the name of the subject. All art that implicitly values the so-called 'mind', the res cogitans, against sensual perception (conceptual art, a lot of installation art) is yet another outgrowth of this moment.

Perhaps the second large restructuring of the constitutional economy of the self was the shock generated by Darwin, who published first his Origin in 1859 and then his Descent of Man in 1871. At once, the 'human', privileged and set apart in the Genesis as special creation, became merely one more 'animal'. Soul, rational or otherwise, lost its rhetorical right to existence. Atheism became more plausible. This was the beginning of the rise of immanence. This was also the period in which ethnology and anthropology crystallized into their modern form: the study of human groups and their structuring of their lifeworld.

All these changes had a dramatic impact on the certainties of the Western Mind: the twin specters of relativism and nihilism raised their heads. If the human was the product of a blind process like evolution, what could the life of such a creature mean? The great philosopher, of perspectival realities, who confronted the new need to philosophize in the absence of metaphysical grounds and certainties, was of course Nietzsche. The great move away from 'deep art' to art of the surface was launched. The utilitarian visions of the Adolph Loos, and later, the Bauhaus; art as design and design as art, are still alive today. The human as animal meant too, a new interest in the irrational: the absurd nihilism of Dada; the interest in the unconscious of Surrealism. It also meant a new interest in the body which would lead to the 60s practices of body art and performance art.

Our third moment, the logical completion of the chain, in which the 'human' acquires the characteristics of 'machine', had been brewing for a while. Perhaps it began with de la Mettrie's natural extension of Descartes' argument in L'homme machine (1747): if animals were machine, the only thing that prevented humans from being machines too was this insubstantial non-material ghost, the res cogitans. Away with it. Though not immediately appreciated, Darwin's theory too led to the same conclusion, though this became clear only with the development of biochemistry and the notion of a self-replicating molecule. Symbolically perhaps the final nail in the coffin was hammered, by first Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri's proposal that heredity was carried by 'genetic material' in the chromosomes in the early 1900s, and then the famous discovery in the 1950s by Watson and Crick, of the structure of DNA, the reproducing molecule of heredity. A person seemed now reducible to a 'code' in the form of a mere molecule. This set of value transfers, which had been in process for a while, problematized what was held to be the special province of the artist: originality, creativity. Or more precisely: it eroded the dichotomy between the supposed activity of creativity and passivity of interpretation. A natural outcome: the readymades of Duchamp. Found art, Random art, the aesthetics of the frame mentioned earlier. The constructed 'grammar' of Suprematism, machine aesthetics of Futurism, the bare minimalist lines of Mondrian and color-field art. And we continue to draw from this vein of art, produced by a demoted human self to this day...

Those three moments were the stages in the fall of the human. Recent changes, only now beginning to percolate through the world of art, constitute the mirror image of these moments: the rise of the post-human in the rise of the machine.

### Manifesto: An art for the future

Technological change in the past has been exponential; it is no surprise that this acceleration has started off, and completed, another logical chain of movements in the economy of the self, all within the last 50 years. This logical chain is one of the 'promotion' of lower-level entities, the converse of the movement we described as the fall of the human. The repercussions of these changes are still only just unrolling and provide us with a glimpse of the aesthetic horizon of the future-in-the-present.

Our first counter-moment was the rise of systems-thinking in the 60s and 70s. Motivated by analyzing weather patterns, Edward Lorenz in 1961 discovered the Lorenz attractor and with it Chaos Theory. [34] Soon complex systems were analyzed from a 'macro' nonlinear-dynamics perspective in biology, perception and cognition (in the research project on embodied cognition inspired by the work of J.J. Gibson or the neo-Piagetian models of child development) and economics (in the form of econophysics); a perspective that emphasized randomness and large changes in behavior resulting from small changes in stimuli. The discovery of human-caused global climate change at that same time, crystallized in the Keeling curve (1960), only hammered home that large complex systems, like the global climate, economics, mass behavior, have a logic and unpredictability that necessitate study on their own, a kind of complex, supra-individual, macro-behavior, that is emergent from typically simple micro-behavior. Recent proposals in philosophy (e.g by Alva Noë or Evan Thompson) have even situated consciousness not in the individual brain but in 'life' or in the complex dynamic between organism and environment. 'Nature', both the aspect of ourselves that is ineluctably social, and larger totalities of which we are part, has acquired characteristics of the 'human': agency, unpredictability, a systemic logic. No wonder then that this was the moment of Lovelock and Margulis' Gaia hypothesis (1970)! [35]

The social ramifications are becoming clearer: with the rise of 'crowdfunding', social networks, 'crowd-curation' and fake-news phenomena deciding elections, our times have seen an obvious rise in the notion that the creative active unit may not be the individual but the group (with its own unique ecology, that defines it as an ontological unit.) What, then, of art? Claire Bishop (2006) has analyzed the appropriation of social forms, like dancing (Oiticica), politics (Beuys) or running a garage (Martha Rosler) in art practice of the 60s: "[their intent was] to collapse the distinction between performer and audience, professional and amateur, production and reception. Their emphasis is on collaboration, and the collective dimension of social experience." (2006: 10). There have been efforts by scientist-artists like M.M. Al-Rifaie to use dynamical systems or ant-colony optimization algorithms to create so-called 'swarmic art'. The Centre Pompidou in 2017 presented its curated collection of collaborative art (it was mostly art done by collectives) entitled Cosmopolis #1: Collective Intelligence.

But surely this is the tip of the iceberg. What can the transcendent artwork be so that it disturbs us away from the prison of our self-conceptions and individual life histories, to this notion of the self as distributed over many persons? Perhaps even the self as a distributed network of entities within one person? Art that blurs those ancient dichotomies of inside/outside, private/public, individual/community. Its awe-inspiring (tremendum) dimension has to highlight the loss of individual control, the inability to plan and order our activity because of agency resting in a larger collective of which we form a small part. Its fascination: the liberation from our individual imaginations, our merging into larger wills and movements, the acceptance of systemic unpredictability (and perhaps thus a renunciation not of the utopian impulse, but of the many disastrous attempts at realizing determinate utopian social projects all predicated on systemic predictability.) A mysterious art of the dissolution of atomic individualism, concerned not with re-asserting our pitiful social identities but with a future that affects us as collectivities: with migration patterns, with climate change, with population phenomena. And the artist? We dream of an artwork created by the action of an entire community, an entire society. Of art commissioned and owned by crowds. Of art curated by the millions. Of the truly collaborative art of the future...

The rise of the PC, the mobile device and the internet which started in the 80s, and seems slowly to be saturating every aspect of our lives, has transformed our understanding of what is natural. When every aspect of our lives (shopping, dating, gossiping, sex, playing) is mediated by a technological device, the distinction between 'nature' and 'machine', or real and the virtual becomes fuzzy. When the sexual drive, thought of as the very epitome of our embodied animalistic selves, is focused more towards a stream of mechanical [36] information on a porn website on a cellphone screen, than towards another warm body, to what extent has it already partaken greatly of the mechanical? When our memories are extended on our Facebook wallposts, where do they live? In our brains? Surely not, we have forgotten the mnemonic content! In the virtual cloud? But clouds don't have memory, because memory is not merely record but also purposive and intentional retrieval. Enter the new subject, the cyborg that is the amalgam of human and the cellphone. Enter artificial life. 'Machines' are our new 'Nature', as much part of our daily drives and memories as our bodies: our selves are already cyborg, already virtual.

With even the Tate Modern website including 'virtual reality' into its hallowed pantheon of art, Virtual and Augmented Reality as media for the creation of artworks is rapidly gaining acceptance. From its pioneering phase in the 90s with Char Davies' Osmose, to the recent spate of work's [sic] developed for the Oculus Rift headset, including Jeremy Couillard's phantasmagoric 2015 installation at the Miami Beach Art Basel, most of this work highlights the 'immersive', another name for a feeling of embodiment without the body. And some artists, like the Dutch group Art Tower, even exhibit their work primarily at exhibitions in a virtual world like Second Life. Another trend is the integral use of technology to create art: not simply digital art but also physical artworks created by programmable painting machines. But neither the nascent development of a new media, nor an uncritical aesthetics of immersion in a sound and light show, can make for an art of the future.

For that, this new self (the virtual avatar, the self stripped of embodiment, whose nature is the machine, the cyborg, the extended mind (as theorized by Andy Clark and David Chalmers), whose self is distributed over the human body and the bodies of the technologies that form an integral part of its everyday function), this new future self has to be intimated to us by the transcendent artwork. Transcendence here: the horror of disembodiment, the loss of instincts developed through evolutionary interaction with a material universe, and the fascination of a potential liberation from the physical environment. The danger and the allure. To insist on materiality, to romanticize the physical and shun digital art as some nostalgic art critics and curators seem to be doing, is but an ostrich tactic of hiding one's head when the lions attack. It would be like those few imagined bards or potential mathematicians who at the dawn of that other technology of virtual presence and mediation, writing, turned their backs on the reed pen, and were lost to history. Change must be faced; and that doesn't mean one has to extol the seemingly inevitable, or in Foster's acid formulation 'surf the dialectic' either. It is precisely the new naturalness of technological mediation, of the cyborg self, that this new art must make visible. We dream of an art that exploits to the hilt the social possibilities of virtuality; that doesn't simply replicate existing physical institutions in a virtual world, but allows us to sense already in the present the strange ecstasies and grim dissolutions that lie in wait for us in that ancient wish, now future reality: bodiless presence.

The third most radical shock to the conceptual basis of the layered self is in process as we speak, with the dramatic rise of the use and scope of artificial intelligence. Suddenly [37], at least behaviorally, machines have started to acquired [sic] humans skills at near-human competencies: they plan, they perform complex tasks like driving, they trade stocks, and are poised to replace most of human labor. And of course, each year brings yet another landmark: in 2016 self-taught algorithm AlphaGo defeated the leading human player in a game in which several experts had predicted human would always retain the upper hand. The age of the machine is just beginning; and as machines become more intelligent than us, we might soon be relegated to cadet status in a world we once thought we were the lords of, by virtue of our intelligence.

Art has barely begun to encounter this new change: artists have been using A.I. integrally in their works only for the past 2 years or so. After all it was only in 2015 that Google's DeepDream showed us the very strange visual representations A.I. algorithms formed of common objects. Since then, most of the work in this area uses algorithms called GANs (generative adversarial networks) which, after the studying of a large number of images, is able to produce original images like the category it has studied. For instance, Mario Klingemann, a pioneer in this area, has built an algorithm that has studied the poses in a huge database of classic paintings, and given any new pose it has not necessarily studied, is able to produce a painting of a nude in that pose in a strange style of its own. In any case this an area that will clearly grow in conceptual depth and aesthetic quality: AI painters will soon be sold along with their paintings in galleries. These paintings resuscitate old questions in aesthetics: what is creativity? If an algorithm is better than a human at producing mnemonic art, which we referred to in the first part of this essay, then what can the human continue to do? One way forth is to move away from the purely cognitive and the mnemonic and to focus on the ethical and the affective, both areas which seem very remote from the mechanizable. It is partly this idea that has motivated our notion of transcendent art.

But once again development of a new media (of creating AI artists who then create art, or using the AI to 'enhance human capabilities'), or even of redefining the domain of the human in the aesthetic, is only a small part of the need of a future art. There is a larger need for art to enable us to transcend our empirical selves, to become our future selves watched over by super-intelligent machines, but in a way that allows for human flourishing as well. If we, as we always have, at least in the Western philosophical tradition, attribute greater value and even greater being to our cognition (the standard philosophical grace which supposedly explains our superiority to animals), won't the super-intelligent machines of the future have to be allowed more worth and more being than we possess? The future artwork has to present us with the tremendum of a life where we live with our machines as our animals live under us; the fascinans of a life where Marx's dream of liberation from labor is realized (though the concomitant unemployment and loss of purpose might prove a nightmare indeed). Art can console, art can shock us into more actively shaping the technological explosion that we have passively allowed to override our human needs and pull us along, like so much flotsam. The future art has to perform both duties.

So far we have focused on thematic/material concerns and the new media of the art of the future. A few words should be said about the formal, though predicting or even calling for styles of the future is a hazardous venture at best, style being so topical, so individual.

First the demands of the mysterium, of an art resistant to appropriating discourse, rules out any art that can be replaced by its description; out goes the 'black paint' elevated to art by the theory tacked on to it by the likes of Danto. However hard it may be to talk about, we need to resuscitate a focus on the aesthetic, sensual qualities of the work of art, and not highlight, as its ultimate significance, its conceptual content; we need to start to revalue not merely pure denotative signification, but the structure and affective quality of the sign and its ramifying connotations. Most 'installation art', which tends to put the stress on its animating concept, will not fulfill the need. Though it is somewhat ridiculous to prescribe the affective quality of a sign, we will hazard a wish, merely in the nature of a daydream. The aesthetic emotions produced by the transcendent artwork are ambiguous, disturbing ones: the estranging awe and the attracting beauty; emotions that keep faith with the unquiet core of the self.

Secondly, any sort of presence that the artwork has cannot be of the devouring kind that (analyzed as the 'modeling aesthetic' by Adrian Stokes) refers ultimately to a foundational Absolute, a source of all presence. Instead, the artwork which has the future as its model of the transcendent object, has to be relational, a delicate interpellation that is only answered by the viewer's response. Not 'facingness' (Thierry de Duve), but a face: facingness is bare presence; the other's face creates and calls the self into relation. That is, like the 'ostensive image' of Rancière (2007: 23), but not entirely stripped of signification and not grounded in the perfections of the dynamical sublime and the absolute. Some examples to make this abstract distinction clearer: Within the aesthetic of the 'formless', not the massive painting as fetish or psychoanalytic session by Cy Twombly, but the small tentative gestures at nascent form done on old bills of lading by Martha Jungwirth. Within the art inspired by the color-field movement, not the complacent wisdom of S.H. Raza, but the self-ground colors (impossible to capture in a digital image) and the strange flirting with faux-structuralist forms of Tanya Goel. Not Michelangelo but Giorgione; the enigmatic free emptinesses of the early de Chirico, not the overdetermined rational irrationality of Dalí. The pulpy abjections of a Guston, not the bleak autonomy of a Motherwell. Not the overwhelming bare presence of the iconostasis underlined with gold, nor the bare presence without theological content of say Barnett Newman. To point it up sharply: the enigmatic homemade relational ethics of the typically small artefact-paintings by the young artist Ezra Tessler, as against the nonrelational engulfing monumentality of say so many late-Rothko [38] surfaces, or the made-for-branding pop-hip immanence of someone like Yayoi Kusama.

To elaborate: consider for instance two works of art, both confined to the general formal space of 'color field'. Rothko's 'Green and Tangerine on Red' hangs in the The Rothko Room at the Phillips Collection in Washington D.C. It's hung close to the floor and is a massive painting (94'' x 69'') of two large rectangles, one a dark brooding green and below it an intense saturated orange, both on a brownish ground. The colors engulf the field of vision. Encountering the painting is an experience of the kind of transcendence that is wholly other, that only be called [sic] Absolute: the painting does not need the viewer, it radiates aseity like the God that Moses encountered in the burning bush. As Jeffrey Weiss puts it, the encounter "a quasi-religious experience of limitless immensity [...] the canvasses also produce [their] own sacrosanct space." (Weiss, 1998). In other words an experience of the mathematical sublime. Now consider Ezra Tessler's quirkily named "Little private wharf". Made of three pieces of funnily shaped flat wood panels, colored red, green and greenish yellow, the whole somewhat resembles a jigsaw piece. There is a homemade look about it, a naïveté that could be alienating in a product sense. Though it is a surface, and thus presumable a painting and not a sculpture, it is not an image consumable on the net, it moves out of the wall. Indeed its indicated dimensions are 4" x 4" x 4": in other words its not merely an image, but an artefact. It invites contemplation, never frightening the viewer into submission. Its name invites and frustrates comprehension. It enters into a relation with the viewer, asking him to move around it, ponder its name, be fascinated by its incompleteness, yet somewhat estranged by its makeshift character. All this, yet it seems to partake of the transcendent, in our sense: not God in the Burning Bush or in the voice after the whirlwind, but the creative-created god of the prayer of 'Ibn Arabi. Not the kind of Absolute evoked by Rothko, but merely something that asks more of the viewer, asks her to be more than she is, asks for a relation...

### Envoi

Insofar as the future is a vision, an adventure into the unknown, it is transcendent. Insofar as it is fate, it is already immanent in the present. We need an unsettling art that faces the transcendent future, that allows us to shape and be shaped by our future. If this sounds messianic, it is: the Messiah after all is not some special human, stamped with God's special seal of approval, come to rescue us. She can only be the massive figure, the pattern in time we create, driven by our fragile utopian impulses: she is created by our ability to look beyond our noses into the future in active engagement with our lives, individual and collective. The Messiah is not a human, nor a machine; she is the project of potential humanity.

### Mark of the Conceptual?

Karthik Kalyanaraman and Raghava KK

Ever since Marcel Duchamp, the grandfather of contemporary art in the 1950s, defended his practice as "mental", as against mere "retinal" art, which pleases the eye, the opposition between a supposedly less intellectual retinality and a more philosophical "conceptuality" has become entrenched in the visual arts. To prove one's artistic raison d'être, artists now need to talk "ideas". What was a moment of artistic self-assertion by Duchamp has become a dogma, endlessly regurgitated in exhibition catalogues, art magazines and curatorial statements.

But, since things are not ideas, what makes one art object or process seem more "conceptual" than another? What is the mark of the conceptual? Now that we have about 70 years of these ideas embedded in art practice, forming a "tradition" of sorts, one can begin to ponder over this question. But as a tradition grows, it grows old: What starts as revolution becomes authority and innovations become habits. The retinal has its clichés, but we are now also quite familiar with the longueurs of the conceptual. So the same question, viewed from a different perspective, becomes: What are the tricks of the trade by which conceptuality is gestured at in contemporary art?

One can catalogue them: the usually standardized ways to employ visual reduction, metaphor and metonymy in conjunction, which we have all seen often enough. For instance, consider the alteration of a modernist masterpiece as "homage" (Sylvie Fleury's furry Mondrians, Ai Weiwei's Hanging Man), or outsourcing of the production of art as a sign of "renouncing" authorship (Tobias Rehberger), or artwork with high production value "conceived" by the artist and made in a nameless factory (most famous artists of today), or scale changes for shock and awe (so many artists, alas, but Phyllida Barlow, for instance), or ornamenting traditional craft with a modernist bell and a minimalist whistle (an Indian malaise), or "curation" and display of sociological data (Gillian Wearing), or.... But we have chosen another mode of doing art criticism.

Not enough art criticism acknowledges its provisionality, its tentativeness, perhaps in its attempt to gain a sort of power over the artist, a quarrel as old as the one between Plato and Homer or between Abhinavagupta's interlocutors and the dramatists. Artists have evaded this struggle by producing their own art criticism, or in some instances (Duchamp himself comes to mind), passing off (typically either derivative or wilfully absurd) theory as art.

We, on the other hand, have tried, as performance and experiment, art criticism in the very medium of its object. In a sense, we can think of this as a sort of return to the last great work, Mnemosyne, of Alois Riegl, a founder of the discipline of art history. [The Mnemosyne Atlas was Aby Warburg's last work, not Riegl's; the attribution is as printed in the source.]

We ask you to contemplate the following pages keeping in mind that each artwork is simultaneously parodic and absolutely earnest, in other words, ironic, even self-ironic. Each work stands at a cusp, a threshold, pointing at both the "real world" of issues and the genre of which it is a part. We might have sounded critical of contemporary practice, but while looking at these images, we would like you to ask yourself, what makes for richness in such art? We have a provisional test we would like to suggest: What would make you want to re-encounter an artwork you have already met? What would it be for an artwork to demand that renewal of acquaintance, in a world where most visuality is one-use only? Is it the strength of the artist's desire to speak to you? Is it something ethical? Might it be conceptual depth? If so, in what does that consist? Or, an impish thought, might retinality have something to do with it after all?

### Metacritical Epilogue

Most criticism seems to pretend, as a rhetorical move, that it speaks from the position of absolute truth. We want to expose to you, our readers, our own biases, to the extent we can see them clearly. We do not trust in the percept-concept dichotomy, that Cartesian dualism of subject and object or the Kantian one of phenomenon-noumenon. We do not think there is anything that is retinal that is not conceptual. And vice versa. If forced to adopt a dualism, Karthik will perhaps adopt the Sankhyan one, where pure consciousness is alien to all else, and percept and concept only so many material modes for its self-recognition. Raghava will probably blur the differences by performing his own Pragmatism.

A note on the piece Ahyu ma khalaqtum!, page 93: What constitutes good and bad appropriation in the arts? Appropriation, or influence, is a necessary part of all art. But today, our Left unproblematically cites "appropriation" as accusation, even granted there is such a thing as misappropriation. Were the Shattaris, a militant and once powerful order among the Indian Sufis, wrong in "appropriating" yoga for their dhikr*? Were the American slaves wrong in appropriating the Bible for their songs of liberation? Was the Christian appropriation of the Tanakh (Hebrew scriptures) justified? Does it make a difference that one of the two artists who made this is deeply influenced by Islam and is a student of Arabic and the Quranic heritage? These questions are not rhetorical.

*Dhikr, which literally means remembrance, refers to Islamic spiritual practices such as repeated recitations of scriptural passages. The practice of dhikr is central to Sufi worship and can also include music, dance and meditation.
