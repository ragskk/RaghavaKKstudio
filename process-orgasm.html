How an orgasm became a painting.

A record of the process behind La petite mort. The artist's orgasm was recorded as brainwave data, visualised neuron by neuron, interpreted by hand, and returned to oil on canvas by a robot and a painter working together. Five steps, shown plainly.

PrologueThe premise

What cannot be digitised?

Burning Man, 2017. After a heartbreaking divorce, Raghava went to the desert and came back with a two-hundred-year plan for crypto art: a manifesto hybridised onto a business plan, imagining a decentralised global repository of human imagination. The polarised worlds of art and business would not come together to build it.

What survived was a question, sharpened in conversation with his brother, the critic Karthik Kalyanaraman: everything seemed digitisable, so everything seemed sellable. Then what is the craziest thing one could sell? The most intimate, personal, non-sellable item imaginable.

The answer was the orgasm: la petite mort, the little death, the one experience where the mental and the physical meld. The project takes a physical, emotional, visceral experience, turns it into a digital record, and then brings it back into the physical world as a fresh sensual encounter.

01
The Idea2017 → 2019

An announcement, two years early.

The idea travelled as a spoken promise before it was a work. In 2019, on stage at the FofX festival in Uttarakhand, Raghava and the neuroscientist Abhijeet Satani announced that they would one day capture Raghava's orgasm and turn it into art.

The promise · FofX Festival · 2019“One day soon, we shall capture our orgasm and turn it into art.”
02
Data CollectionBengaluru · 2021

The orgasm is recorded as brainwaves.

With equipment developed by Abhijeet Satani, the studio in Bengaluru became a recording room. Electrodes were placed, the room prepared, and the capture rehearsed, first with music and painting, to test how the brain read on the machine, before the orgasm itself was recorded as neural data.

Primitive visualisation of the recorded orgasm · Bengaluru 2021 · Video: Abhijeet Satani

03
Data VisualisationMumbai · 2021

Every neuron becomes a brush stroke.

Harshit Agrawal brought the neuro-data into Blender and rebuilt it in three dimensions. The rule was set here, and it carries through to the finished painting: each firing neuron is turned into an individual brush stroke. The data was also rendered procedurally, and exported as a 3D brain object the artist could turn in his hands.

The visualised brain, turning · 2021 · Video: Harshit Agrawal

04
Creative InterpretationNew York · 2021

The artist chooses his angle.

In New York, the science hands over to the hand. Raghava selected a single viewing angle of the brain from the 3D file, then composed the artwork in layers: the tangle of neural threads, and the garden of flowers and forms that grows on it. The data decides the structure; the artist decides everything else.

05
Digital to PhysicalNew York · 2021

A robot and a painter, on the same canvas.

At the Artmatr lab, the digital file was returned to matter. Special oil pigments were prepared in a material-science lab set up by Artmatr in Israel. The contours of the artwork were turned into G-code for the robots. Then months of experiments in mark making, testing how a machine lays paint and how a hand answers it, before the final works were painted and printed, Raghava working alongside the robots.

The Artwork · Sotheby's HQ, New York · October 2021

La petite mort.

The finished works: one large canvas and three studies, oil on canvas, painted and printed at the Artmatr lab. Launched at Sotheby's HQ, New York, in October 2021, alongside Boundless Space: The Possibilities of Burning Man, the charitable auction produced by Sotheby's and the Burning Man Project.

One physical, private experience now exists as data, as a 3D object, as a digital composition, and as oil on canvas, each a different body for the same fifteen seconds. The record can keep taking new forms; that is the point of having made it.

BeyondThe work stays alive

After the canvas, the record keeps going.

Because the source is data, the work does not have to end at the wall. An AI that studied multiple orgasms generated new, synthetic ones, a Fake Male Orgasm and a Fake Female Orgasm: a future series grown from the same method. And the installation itself was designed to keep a robot in the room: a slow-moving arm built for human collaboration, sketched for the Sotheby's floor.

CodaOne series, one process

This process belongs to the Orgasm Project alone.

Nothing here is a method. The electrodes, the one-neuron-one-stroke rule, the robots, the pigments made in a lab: these were invented for this work and end with it. Each series begins by building its own process.
